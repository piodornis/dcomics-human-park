## 1. Establish the page's job

Before dividing a page into panels, state internally what the page must accomplish. Examples:

- introduce a location
- deliver a conversation
- escalate a chase
- hide information from one character
- reveal a discovery to the reader
- create a pause after a major event
- end on a page-turn question

Panel design should serve that job.

## 2. Separate beats from panels

A story beat is not automatically one panel.

A beat may need:

- one large panel for impact
- multiple panels for reaction and timing
- an insert to clarify an object
- a silent panel to create emotional space

Likewise, multiple low-importance actions may fit in one panel if clarity is preserved.

## 3. Control information order

For every panel ask:

1. What does the reader notice first?
2. What does the reader notice second?
3. Who knows what at this moment?
4. Is the panel revealing information too early?
5. Does the next panel depend on this information?

Use composition to control information rather than adding exposition.

## 4. Preserve spatial logic

Across sequential panels:

- keep entrances and exits consistent
- avoid unexplained left/right reversals
- establish geography before complex movement
- use wider shots when spatial confusion is likely
- return to an establishing view after major relocation when needed

Breaking orientation can be useful for disorientation, but it should be deliberate.

## 5. Match panel size to importance

Panel size affects perceived time and emphasis.

General tendency:

- larger panel = more attention, weight, scale, or pause
- smaller panel = quicker beat, detail, or compressed action

Do not use this mechanically. Dialogue density and page composition also matter.

## 6. Use reaction panels selectively

Reaction panels are useful when a change in understanding matters.

Avoid adding reaction panels merely to increase panel count.

A reaction is especially valuable when:

- a relationship shifts
- a lie is detected
- a reveal lands differently for two characters
- the reader needs time to register consequences

## 7. Plan dialogue before composition is locked

Estimate balloon count and reading order early.

Check:

- first speaker appears in a natural reading position
- balloons can flow without crossing tails unnecessarily
- faces and key hand actions remain visible
- captions do not compete with the visual focal point

If the script has too much text for the panel, report the layout pressure.

## 8. Use page turns intentionally

Strong page-turn candidates include:

- first appearance of a major character
- discovery
- reveal of scale
- reversal
- threat
- punchline
- visual contrast
- sudden location change

Do not manufacture artificial suspense if it distorts the story.

## 9. Distinguish technical note from image description

Technical note:

`Medium close-up, slight low angle, A foreground right, B soft in background.`

Natural image description:

`A looks up from the broken console while B stands behind them in the doorway, waiting for an answer.`

Use both when the storyboard may later support image generation or an artist handoff.

## 10. Identify visual continuity dependencies

Flag details that must carry across pages or issues, such as:

- injuries
- damaged clothing
- props in hand
- open doors
- vehicle position
- lighting state
- weather
- time of day
- dirt, blood, water, or debris
- equipment transformations

Do not assume these reset between scenes.

## 11. Escalate story problems instead of solving them silently

If the script cannot be staged clearly without changing story content:

1. preserve the script as the baseline
2. explain the staging problem
3. label a possible fix `PROPOSAL`
4. offer the smallest effective change first

Examples:

- split one overloaded panel into two
- move one line of dialogue to the adjacent panel
- add a silent reaction panel
- change page break

Only propose plot or dialogue rewriting when simpler visual solutions fail.
